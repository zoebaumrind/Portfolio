I Saw the Clips.

I love Saturday Night Live, but I rarely watch a full episode anymore. I keep up with the cast through Instagram Reels and send sketches to friends I know will get the joke. I’d still call myself a fan.

A Culture Lab project by Zoe Baumrind

Independent Culture Lab project. An original editorial exploration combining cultural observation, published industry research, public video data, interactive storytelling, and entertainment brand strategy.

I still feel like a fan

I’m usually the person telling someone they need to watch something. Lately the answer is “I saw some clips,” and sometimes I realize that’s how I’ve been keeping up, too. In the same week I might want to know what happened on SNL, why a podcast host is getting dragged, and which song everyone is suddenly obsessed with. I don’t have time for all of it. A sketch fits into the few minutes I have, and I can send it to a friend without either of us having seen the episode. That’s still time the show gets to spend in our lives.

How people keep up

Deloitte found that 71% of Gen Z fans said their fandom often led them across platforms, compared with 55% of fans overall. And 44% of all fans said they discover content on social media and go somewhere else to watch, listen to, or buy the full version. That’s self-reported behavior across several kinds of entertainment. It gives a sense of how easily a fan’s experience can outgrow any one platform.

There’s also a reason to take the shorter experience seriously on its own. In YouTube and Ipsos’s 2023 research, 54% of online adults aged 18–44 said they’d prefer a creator’s breakdown of a major event to the event itself. A breakdown is different from a sketch, but I recognize the impulse. Sometimes the version that fits into your day is the version you actually want. Research notes and sources

Sampling and metric definitions

What happens when you make it for the clip?

A song can be great for seven seconds on TikTok and still feel underwhelming when you hear the whole thing. A trailer can sell you on a tone the movie barely has. A podcast clip can make a two-hour conversation sound like it’s all fights. Those cuts can do a great job of getting attention and still set the wrong expectation for what comes next.

I’m more interested in what happens before the edit. If the sharpest line keeps getting the most reach, I can imagine a producer pushing for more confrontation in the next interview. If the social team needs a scene that works with no setup, there’s a temptation to write for that moment from the start. A catchy hook or a strong payoff can make the whole thing better. I’d worry when the moment meant for the feed starts deciding what happens in the rest of the work.

Halsey said in 2022 that their label wanted a viral TikTok moment before releasing a song. That’s pressure around a release. Nick Polak and Julian Schaap got closer to the creative question by interviewing 20 professional musicians and songwriters. Some saw a commercial reason to shape music for platforms, and the people interviewed differed in how far they’d go. That’s enough to take the pressure seriously. Whether it makes the work worse depends on what changes and what gets lost.

What I’d tell an entertainment brand

Say someone watches SNL sketches every week and never subscribes to Peacock. NBCUniversal could still earn ad revenue on eligible clips or keep a recurring character familiar between seasons. That person could also introduce a friend to the show or later show up to a live event. Those are different kinds of value. The platform may keep part of the revenue and much of the audience data, and a meme can travel without anyone knowing where it came from. I’d judge each clip by the part it plays in keeping the show in people’s lives and check what the company can actually see or earn.

Three ways people can have a relationship with the same show

Someone can watch the full finale, keep up through clips the following week, and send a sketch to a friend either time. I’d use these as behaviors to investigate. A show’s public numbers don’t tell us how big each group is or where they overlap.

What would you ask the clip to do?

Proposed brand tests. Choose an objective to see what to cut, where to publish it, how to judge the result, and what to watch for.

I’d run a short SNL pilot with standalone sketches and episode promos. Compare each with similar posts from the same account and keep the results apart: clip revenue after editing and promotion costs where it’s visible, repeat clip viewing, brand-linked sharing, and qualified episode starts where they can be attributed. If the sketches pay for themselves or keep fans coming back, that gives the team a reason to keep making them even if the promos drive more starts. If one sketch takes off, I’d use that to decide what to test next in distribution. I’d want more evidence before using it to tell a writer how to write the next sketch.

Sources and methods

How the evidence fits together

The published studies describe specific populations and behaviors. The cases document examples of distribution and cultural circulation. The viewer paths illustrate possibilities, and the brand tests are recommendations. Together they support a question about how people participate. They give no estimate of how many SNL fans primarily watch clips and no causal estimate of loyalty or conversion.

Published research and populations

Deloitte, 2026. An online study of 3,575 U.S. consumers in October–November 2025, weighted to Census benchmarks. The fan subset identified as a fan in at least one of nine categories. Among those fans, 73% of Gen Z and 52% overall named social media their main discovery route. The cross-platform figures are self-reports, with no tracking from a particular clip to the full work. Deloitte Digital Media Trends, 2026

YouTube and Ipsos, 2023. The event-breakdown question measured stated preference among 25,892 online adults ages 18–44 in 14 countries. In the same sample, 68% reported watching subjects they liked across multiple formats. In a separate age subgroup, 47% of online 18–24-year-olds (n=6,982) reported watching fan-made videos in the previous year. The research was commissioned by YouTube. YouTube Culture & Trends Report

More context. Deloitte’s 2025 report found that 56% of Gen Z respondents considered social media content more relevant than traditional content, in a study of 3,595 U.S. consumers. Edison reported that 26% of Gen Z past-month podcast listeners named social posts as their main discovery route, compared with 28% for YouTube search. Both results cover behavior broader than clipping. Deloitte, 2025 · Edison Research at SSRS

Other ways clips travel

SNL’s “Domingo” sketch works as a standalone story. The character then turned up in a Sabrina Carpenter concert appearance. Twitch lets viewers choose the moment, and TikTok’s Yeehaw Challenge is an example of fans making something with the original solo “Old Town Road”. Instagram’s Features test explores an in-app route from a Reel to a longer video.

Hot Ones sampling and public view counts

The November 23, 2026 snapshot contains 19 official First We Feast Shorts matched by identifiable guest or episode to six official full uploads from Hot Ones and Hot Ones Versus. The episodes were released October 29–November 20, 2026. Paired Shorts appeared within seven days of release. The convenience sample includes promotional cuts and excerpts. Each episode count appears once per Short in the CSV, so deduplicate episodes before aggregating.
